Reading the board: spread flags & the Brier score

What is a spread flag? A flag appears when the model's spread disagrees with the Vegas spread by 3 or more points. Inside about 3 points, model and market disagreeing is just noise — different rounding, a bit of public money, a line that moved an hour ago. At 3+ points, one side is making a real claim the other rejects, and the flag names the team the model thinks is mispriced.

Why flags happen. The model only eats statistics, so it can be slow on things Vegas prices instantly — a midweek injury, a quarterback quietly playing hurt. Or the reverse: Vegas shades popular teams because it knows the public will bet them anyway, and a stats-only model refuses to pay that tax. Every flag is one of those two stories. The weekly grading exists to find out which story is usually true.

A flag can oppose the model's own pick. The pick answers "who wins?" — the flag answers "where is the price wrong?" In Week 6 the model picked the Rams to beat Arizona and flagged Arizona getting 12.5 points as the mispriced side. Arizona won outright. The pick lost; the flag was the better read. That is why the two are graded separately.

How flags are graded. Against the closing Vegas line, every Tuesday, published win or lose — Week 6 went 6/7. One week proves nothing: the standing rule here is three straight weeks at 55% or better before a flag record counts as a real edge, and a flag is never, by itself, a betting recommendation. It does not know what price you got, or what you can afford to lose.

What is the Brier score? The record tells you whether the pick won. The Brier score tells you whether the confidence was earned. For every game: take the model's win probability for the team that actually won, subtract 1, square it, then average over all games.

Call a game at 80% and win: (0.80 − 1)² = 0.04, an excellent mark. Call it at 80% and lose: (0.80 − 0)² = 0.64 — the square makes confident misses cost far more than confident hits earn. Shrug 50/50 on everything and you score exactly 0.25, the coin-flip baseline. The scale runs 0 (perfect) to 1 (perfectly wrong), lower is better.

Week 6 scored 0.226 — better than the coin-flip line, honestly short of sharp. One game shows why the number matters: Kansas City at 82% winning was worth 0.03, while the Rams at 66% losing cost 0.44 on its own. A model can post a winning record and still be badly calibrated; the Brier score is how you catch that.
